\section{Evaluation (Preliminary)}\label{sec:evaluation}

This section reports what has been \emph{measured} as of this draft. It is marked preliminary deliberately: the formal cross-platform benchmark sweep --- with controlled ambient and calibrated instrumentation --- has not yet been run. Every number below is reproducible from the repository as it stands; nothing here is projected or estimated. Section~\ref{sec:eval-gaps} states plainly what is \emph{not} yet claimed.

\subsection{Firmware Footprint}

The ESP32-C3 firmware was built in all three modes (Section~\ref{sec:deployment}) and measured with \texttt{idf.py size-files}. Table~\ref{tab:footprint} gives the result. The whole image comfortably fits the 1\,MB application partition in every mode.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Build mode} & \textbf{Firmware image} & \textbf{App partition free} \\
\midrule
\texttt{STANDALONE}        & 213 KB & 79\% of 1 MB \\
\texttt{REPLAY\_STANDALONE} & 164 KB & 84\% of 1 MB \\
\texttt{HIL\_PERIPHERAL}    & 212 KB & 79\% of 1 MB \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{ESP32-C3 firmware footprint, measured per build mode.}
\label{tab:footprint}
\end{table}

The budget that matters for portability is not the whole image --- most of it is ESP-IDF and FreeRTOS --- but the contribution of the portable \texttt{core/} and \texttt{protocol/} sources, which the PRD caps at 64\,KB \texttt{.text} and 16\,KB \texttt{.bss}. In \texttt{STANDALONE}, where the ESP32 runs the control law, \texttt{core/} contributes \textbf{11{,}728\,bytes} of \texttt{.text} and \textbf{0\,bytes} of \texttt{.bss} --- under one fifth of the text budget, with no static RAM at all, consistent with the no-heap, no-static-state discipline. In \texttt{HIL\_PERIPHERAL}, where the host runs the core, \texttt{core/} dead-strips entirely and only the \texttt{protocol/} wire codec links, at \textbf{946\,bytes}. A per-build size-budget gate enforces these limits in CI.

As a post-v1 portability exercise, the same \texttt{core/} source has been cross-built \texttt{STANDALONE} for the WCH CH32V003 --- a roughly ten-cent RISC-V (RV32EC) microcontroller with 16\,KB of flash and 2\,KB of SRAM. A compile-time \emph{tiny profile} overrides the static maxima down to a single zone, sensor, and actuator and shrinks the reserved \texttt{thermal\_core\_t} from 4\,KB to 1\,KB, so the core state fits the 2\,KB SRAM. The whole \texttt{STANDALONE} firmware --- the portable core, a bit-banged DS18B20 plus TIM2-PWM and EXTI-tachometer BSP, and the \texttt{ch32fun} runtime --- links at \textbf{13{,}300\,bytes} of flash (81.2\,\% of 16\,KB) and \textbf{1{,}044\,bytes} of SRAM; with the optional UART telemetry tap, fan-health detector, and telemetry-drop counter compiled in, \textbf{15{,}428\,bytes} of flash (94.2\,\% of 16\,KB) and \textbf{1{,}444\,bytes} of SRAM. The command/bypass bench image keeps fan-health compiled out by default and links at \textbf{15{,}092\,bytes} of flash (92.1\,\%) and \textbf{1{,}512\,bytes} of SRAM. All are cross-build measurements gated in CI. Beyond the cross-build, the port has been brought up on real hardware --- the regulator running standalone on the chip --- as reported in Section~\ref{sec:eval-ch32-hw}.

\subsection{Build Determinism}

Determinism is a stated design goal: identical inputs must produce bit-identical outputs. It is verified by a CI gate over the seven non-CAN canonical scenarios. Each scenario is driven through the daemon under a deterministic scenario clock, and the SHA-256 of the captured telemetry CSV is compared:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Run-to-run:} each scenario is run twice; the two CSVs must be byte-identical.
    \item \textbf{Cross-compiler:} the daemon and plant model are rebuilt under GCC and under Clang; the CSVs from the two toolchains must be byte-identical.
\end{itemize}

Both checks pass for all seven scenarios. Because the core uses only Q16.16 fixed-point arithmetic and fixed-size state, this is the expected result --- but the gate makes any future violation a hard CI failure rather than a silent regression.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Scenario Behavior}\label{sec:eval-scenarios}

The canonical scenarios are also behavioral traces: each one drives the plant model and the governor through a defined disturbance, and the captured telemetry shows the closed loop responding. The figures below are rendered straight from the scenario telemetry CSVs by the figure-regeneration pipeline (\texttt{make -C docs/paper figures}); each caption carries the first eight hex digits of its source CSV's SHA-256, recorded in \texttt{docs/paper/figures/manifest.yaml}, and a CI check fails if a caption and its data drift apart. These are deterministic host-simulator runs --- qualitative evidence that the control law behaves as designed, not calibrated hardware benchmarks.

\texttt{heat\_soak\_ramp} (Figure~\ref{fig:heat-soak-ramp}) ramps the zone temperature upward; the step-wise governor advances through its discrete duty staircase, one step per trip crossing, with the slew limiter bounding every transition.

\texttt{step\_load} (Figure~\ref{fig:step-load}) applies an abrupt temperature step; the governor moves to the duty level for the new temperature within one tick and holds it, and the filtered zone signal tracks the step without oscillation.

These traces confirm the \emph{shape} of the response, not its calibrated magnitude. The quantitative control metrics --- settling time, overshoot, fault-detection latency --- remain evaluation targets (Section~\ref{sec:eval-gaps}).

\subsection{Continuous-Integration Coverage}

The implementation is gated by twenty-three CI jobs spanning build and portability checks (Linux builds under GCC and Clang, a no-heap/no-syscall guard on \texttt{core/}, the three-mode ESP32-C3 build, and the CH32V003 cross-build with its tiny-profile unit run), correctness (unit tests, replay goldens, property-based config and command fuzzing), integration (daemon smoke and integration tests, the scenario suite with and without a virtual CAN bus, and the determinism gate above), and static analysis (\texttt{clang-tidy}, \texttt{cppcheck}, and an AddressSanitizer/UBSan run). A line-coverage report accompanies the suite; at this draft it stands at 90.0\,\% line and 96.3\,\% function coverage over \texttt{core/} and the platform code.

\subsection{Hardware-in-the-Loop Bring-Up}

The HIL loop (Section~\ref{sec:deployment}) was brought up on a bench rig: an ESP32-C3 owning a DS18B20 probe and a Noctua NF-A8 fan, with the Linux daemon running the control law over the USB link. The probe was heated by hand and allowed to cool while the daemon's telemetry was captured. Table~\ref{tab:hil-bringup} lists the trip crossings observed in one heat-and-cool cycle (the three-trip configuration in use at capture time).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{ccl}
\toprule
\textbf{Probe temp.} & \textbf{Fan duty} & \textbf{Event} \\
\midrule
27.5 $^\circ$C & 0   & below first trip --- fan off \\
31.3 $^\circ$C & 100 & 30 $^\circ$C trip --- cooling state 1 \\
45.6 $^\circ$C & 160 & 45 $^\circ$C trip --- cooling state 2 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Trip crossings observed during HIL bench bring-up.}
\label{tab:hil-bringup}

The capture confirms the closed loop end to end: temperature samples flowing from the ESP32 to the host, the step-wise governor producing the expected discrete duty staircase, the slew limiter bounding every transition, hysteresis holding fan state on the way down, and the trips re-arming for a subsequent heat cycle. This is a functional bring-up, not a controlled benchmark --- heat was applied by hand and ambient was not regulated --- but it establishes that the same \texttt{core/} logic exercised by the simulation gates also runs correctly against real hardware.

\subsection{CH32V003 On-Hardware Bring-Up}\label{sec:eval-ch32-hw}

The CH32V003 \texttt{STANDALONE} firmware was flashed to a bench rig (Figure~\ref{fig:ch32-hw}) and run with no host in the control loop: a DS18B20 1-Wire probe feeds \texttt{thermal\_core\_step()} on the chip, which drives a Noctua NF-A8 four-wire fan over PWM with tachometer readback. The host's only role is to log the canonical telemetry CSV emitted by the firmware's UART tap.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ch32v003-hw-setup}
\caption{The CH32V003 \texttt{STANDALONE} bench setup: a roughly ten-cent CH32V003 running thermal-core firmware (USB-C powered), a DS18B20 temperature probe with a 4.7\,k$\Omega$ pull-up, and a Noctua NF-A8 four-wire PWM fan on a separate 12\,V rail --- PWM out from the MCU, tachometer in through a 10\,k$\Omega$ pull-up. A USB-to-serial adapter carries the firmware's UART telemetry to a host PC for logging.}
\label{fig:ch32-hw}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch32-capture} is a telemetry capture of that rig over three hand-applied heat/cool cycles. The DS18B20 was warmed with a hot-air source and allowed to cool; the on-chip regulator drove the fan in response. Zone temperature, fan duty, and tachometer RPM all come from the firmware's own telemetry stream. The trace shows the closed loop running entirely on the microcontroller: the step-wise governor advances its discrete duty staircase as temperature climbs, the measured RPM tracks each duty step, and on each heat-up the zone crosses its \texttt{CRITICAL} trip --- the dashed markers are the safety-override events the firmware logged at those crossings. Temperature reached 95\,$^\circ$C at the highest peak, with the fan held at full duty.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ch32v003-capture}
\caption{CH32V003 \texttt{STANDALONE} on-hardware capture: zone temperature, fan duty, and tachometer RPM over three hand-applied heat/cool cycles, logged from the firmware's UART telemetry tap. The raw capture is committed at \texttt{docs/paper/data/ch32v003-capture.csv}.}
\label{fig:ch32-capture}
\end{figure}

With the stall detector enabled in the configuration, the same firmware was run through a second heat/cool exercise --- and at each heat peak the fan rotor was stopped by hand (Figure~\ref{fig:ch32-stall}). At both stalls the measured RPM collapses and the firmware's stall detector catches it: the shaded bands are the fault lifecycle the firmware logged --- a \texttt{FAULT\_ENTER} event once the stall persisted past the configured window, then \texttt{RECOVERING} and \texttt{FAULT\_CLEAR} once the fan was released and spun back up. The detector's force-to-maximum-duty action fired as well, though it is not visually distinct here: the chip was already at its hottest, so the governor was commanding full duty regardless. The erratic RPM inside the second band is the tachometer reading a hand-gripped rotor. The whole fault subsystem --- detection, the \texttt{NORMAL}\,$\rightarrow$\,\texttt{CRITICAL}\,$\rightarrow$\,\texttt{RECOVERING}\,$\rightarrow$\,\texttt{NORMAL} state machine, the safety action, and recovery --- thus runs on the chip, with no host involved.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ch32v003-stall}
\caption{CH32V003 \texttt{STANDALONE} fan-stall fault: the fan rotor was stopped by hand near each heat peak. The shaded bands are the stall-fault lifecycle (\texttt{FAULT\_ENTER} to \texttt{FAULT\_CLEAR}) recorded in the firmware's event log; the measured RPM collapses inside them. The raw capture is committed at \texttt{docs/paper/data/ch32v003-stall-capture.csv}.}
\label{fig:ch32-stall}
\end{figure}

That same capture also carries the per-tick execution time. A compile-time probe brackets each \texttt{thermal\_core\_step()} call with a SysTick read and logs the cost; across the 340 ticks of the capture the step ran in 134--159\,$\mu$s, median 142\,$\mu$s, on the 48\,MHz core --- about 0.014\,\% of the 1\,s control period. The remainder of each period is the blocking DS18B20 conversion and the idle wait; the control law itself is a vanishingly small slice even on this part.

The post-v1 fan-health detector --- the advisory tach-versus-baseline monitor described in the Component Design section --- is also compiled on this chip, the first MCU build to enable the gate. Its baseline is not hand-authored: a host-driven sweep (a Stage~19 bench mode that exposes the regulator's PWM and tach over a UART command channel) stepped each fan's duty across 1--100\,\% and logged the settled RPM at each point. Two reference fans ship in this repo, each with its own JSON config carrying an 8-point \texttt{fan\_health.baseline} authored from a sweep: a Noctua NF-A8 (the active config) and an Arctic P8 PWM PST (a drop-in alt config). Figure~\ref{fig:ch32-sweep} overlays the two sweeps with their baselines --- the same firmware and the same portable \texttt{core/} handle fans with substantially different curves (the NF-A8 tops out near 2{,}250\,RPM, the Arctic near 3{,}070\,RPM, with a different spin-up characteristic). With the detector and telemetry-drop counter compiled in, the telemetry firmware links at \textbf{15{,}428\,bytes} of flash (94.2\,\% of 16\,KB) on either configuration; the default no-telemetry image and the command/bypass bench image keep the detector compiled out.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ch32v003-sweep}
\caption{PWM-to-RPM sweep, captured on the deployed CH32V003 via the Stage~19 host command channel, for the two reference fans shipped with this repo. Each curve is the full 100-point sweep (1\,\% duty steps with the settled tach RPM at each); the ringed points on each curve are the eight values authored into that fan's \texttt{fan\_health.baseline} --- the spin-up threshold plus the four governor-state duties (100/160/220/255). The Arctic P8's modest dip between duty 28 and 41 is its own unstable spin-up zone; the step-wise governor never holds the fan steady there. Raw CSVs (one run for the NF-A8, three runs for the Arctic) are committed under \texttt{docs/paper/data/}.}
\label{fig:ch32-sweep}
\end{figure}

Like the HIL bring-up above, this is a functional demonstration, not a calibrated benchmark --- the heat and the stalls were both applied by hand and ambient was not regulated. What it establishes is that the same \texttt{core/} source exercised by the simulation gates runs its full closed loop on-chip on a ten-cent part: sensing, the governor, fault detection and recovery, the advisory fan-health detector, and actuator drive, with no host involved.

\subsection{What This Section Does Not Yet Claim}\label{sec:eval-gaps}

To keep the evidence trail honest, the following are explicitly \emph{not} claimed here and await the formal benchmark sweep:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Per-tick execution time.} The step function is now instrumented on one target --- the CH32V003 figure of 134--159\,$\mu$s reported in Section~\ref{sec:eval-ch32-hw} --- but the ESP32-C3 has not been measured, and neither figure has been compared against the timing budget in Table~\ref{tab:timing-budget} under controlled conditions.
    \item \textbf{Acoustic results.} No calibrated sound-pressure-level measurement has been taken; PWM-seconds with and without acoustic masking are not yet reported.
    \item \textbf{Quantitative control metrics.} Settling time, overshoot, and fault-detection latency are listed as evaluation targets in Section~6 but are not yet measured.
    \item \textbf{Cross-platform parity table.} Host-side replay parity is gated in CI --- the host replay binary reproduces a committed golden telemetry CSV byte-for-byte --- but the full host-versus-target byte comparison runs on the bench and is not yet reported here as a table.
\end{itemize}

These gaps are scheduled work; closing them needs the formal benchmark sweep on instrumented hardware. The figure-regeneration pipeline that renders the scenario plots above (Section~\ref{sec:eval-scenarios}) will carry their figures once that data exists.
